How weft works

Finder File   Edit   View   Window 1 Fri 9:41

  1. Open a window.

    It fills the desktop. No dragging, no snapping, nothing to arrange.

  2. Open another. It takes half.

    The focused window gives up half of its space to the new one.

  3. A third splits the long side.

    Weft cuts along whichever side is longer, so windows stay usable shapes.

  4. Moving restructures. It never swaps.

    ⌥⇧H takes Notes one step west in the layout, into a column of its own. Nothing trades places, so nothing shrinks to a quarter.

  5. Every desktop is a workspace.

    ⌥1 to ⌥8 jump between your Mission Control desktops, and each one keeps its own layout.

  6. Mission Control still works.

    These are real macOS desktops, so swipes, ⌃← ⌃→ and full-screen apps work as they always have.

Weft reads the keyboard itself. A shortcut runs the moment you press it, with no process started per keypress. When nothing changes, weft does nothing at all. Your Mac stays quiet.

Everything else.
Built in.

Plug in a display. Weft notices.

Each desktop tiles inside its own display, and west and east pick a display by where it sits. Connect or disconnect one and the layouts follow, with no restart.

Get weft.

Free, open source, and about a minute to set up.

Download WeftBar

macOS 15 or later. Apple silicon and Intel.

  1. 1

    Open the zip

    Drag WeftBar into Applications.

  2. 2

    Open Anyway

    Weft isn't notarised yet. In System Settings, Privacy & Security, click Open Anyway.

  3. 3

    Grant two permissions

    Setup walks you through Accessibility and Input Monitoring for the engine, weftd.

  4. 4

    Start tiling

    Open a window and it finds its place. ⌘K in the menu shows every shortcut.

Prefer Terminal? One command, nothing compiles. 
~ % curl -fsSL --retry 5 --retry-all-errors \
  https://raw.githubusercontent.com/amiralibg/weft/main/scripts/install-release.sh | bash

It downloads the latest release, checks its checksum, installs weftd and weftctl in ~/.local/bin and WeftBar in ~/Applications, then opens Setup. Rather read it first? Take the archive from the releases page and run its install.sh.

Permissions go to the engine. macOS grants them per program, and the program moving your windows is weftd, not the icon in your menu bar.

Updates come from the menu. When a release is out, Update to weft… appears in WeftBar's menu.

Stopping is one click. Quit Weft stops tiling, shortcuts and the menu bar icon together. Open WeftBar to start again.

Weft is young.

It needs real permissions to move your windows. For desktops it uses a few private macOS calls. It tests each one when it starts and checks every result, and if a macOS update breaks one, weft turns it off, tells you, and keeps tiling. The design notes are blunt about what works and what macOS won't allow.
